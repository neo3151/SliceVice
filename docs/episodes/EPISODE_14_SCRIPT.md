# EPISODE 14 SCRIPT: "THE VENTANITA"
**Date:** Feb 22, 1980
**Historical Anchor:** Versailles Restaurant, 3555 SW 8th Street, Little Havana. Founded 1971 by Felipe Valls Sr. By 1980, the undisputed nerve center of the Cuban exile community—where exiled presidents debated with taxi drivers through a walk-up coffee window and nobody asked your last name.

---

**Panel 01**
AXEL: (Caption) Day fifty-three. Two days since the Freedom Tower. Two days since Vance walked away. Two days since I stopped running. I don't know what to do with days that aren't numbered.
AXEL: (Caption) The LeMans sounds like a man coughing up gravel. Third gear is gone. Fourth is theoretical. The alternator whine has become a prayer—a sustained note that means "not yet."

**Panel 02**
AXEL: (Caption) SW 12th Avenue, Little Havana. Reya gave me a name: Elio Valdés. Tire shop with a hand-painted sign that says CAUCHOS Y MAS. "Tires and more." The "more" is the part I need.
ELIO: (Dialogue) "You the one Reya called about?"
AXEL: (Dialogue) "I'm the one with the car that's dying."
ELIO: (Dialogue) "They're all dying. That's why I have a job."

**Panel 03**
AXEL: (Caption) Elio is fifty-something, built like a cinder block, hands permanently stained the color of motor oil. He doesn't ask about the Tape. He doesn't ask about Vance. He puts the LeMans on a lift and looks at the transmission the way a surgeon looks at a wound he's already decided to operate on.
ELIO: (Dialogue) "Whoever rebuilt this tranny last used parts from a Buick and a lawn mower. It's a miracle you made it here."
AXEL: (Dialogue) "Miracles are all I've got."
ELIO: (Dialogue) "Come back in four days. I need to find a donor. Go eat something—you look like the car."

**Panel 04**
AXEL: (Caption) Four days. Four days without the LeMans. I haven't been without the car since January 1st. It's like leaving your spine at the mechanic and walking away vertical on faith.
AXEL: (Caption) I walk east on 8th Street. Calle Ocho. The sidewalk smells like tobacco leaf and café con leche. A radio in a barber shop plays WQBA—somebody arguing about the grain embargo in rapid Spanish. I understand maybe every third word. It doesn't matter. The rhythm of the argument is universal.

**Panel 05**
AXEL: (Caption) Versailles. I've driven past it a thousand times. Never stopped. Couriers don't stop. You drive past things. That's the job. That's the whole identity. But the job is on a lift on SW 12th Avenue with a shattered third gear, so today I stop.
AXEL: (Caption) The ventanita. The walk-up window. Chrome frame, shoulder height, a counter worn smooth by ten thousand elbows. Behind the glass: a woman making cafecito with the focused precision of a chemist.

**Panel 06**
AXEL: (Caption) I order a café. She hands it through the window in a cup the size of a thimble. It's black, sweet enough to make my teeth ache, and strong enough to restart the LeMans' alternator. I drink it in two sips. I order another.
AXEL: (Caption) The men at the ventanita glance at me. Anglo. Young. Out of place. They don't care. The ventanita is neutral ground. You can be anyone here as long as you drink your coffee standing up and don't interrupt the argument.

**Panel 07**
AXEL: (Caption) The argument today is about Carter. About the Olympics. About whether boycotting Moscow will accomplish anything besides making the athletes miserable. An old man in a Panama hat insists that Carter is "too soft on the Soviets." Another man, younger, says the problem isn't Carter—it's that "Americans think diplomacy is a sport."
AXEL: (Caption) I listen. I'm on my third cafecito. My hands are beginning to vibrate. I haven't felt anything below my kneecaps since the second cup.

**Panel 08**
DOÑA CARMEN: (Dialogue) "Oye, Miguelito—"
AXEL: (Caption) She's looking at me. Small, maybe eighty, wearing a floral dress and sandals. Her hair is white and pinned back with a tortoiseshell clip. She's reaching through the ventanita with a pastelito de guayaba wrapped in a paper napkin.
DOÑA CARMEN: (Dialogue) "Estás muy flaco. Come."
AXEL: (Dialogue) "I'm—I'm not Miguelito."
DOÑA CARMEN: (Dialogue, smiling) "I know. But you're too thin. Eat."

**Panel 09**
AXEL: (Caption) I take the pastelito. It's still warm. The pastry flakes in my hand—buttery, golden, with a filling of guava paste that's the color of a sunset filtered through a rose window. I try to pay her. She waves the money away like I've insulted her.
AXEL: (Caption) She talks. I listen. Her Spanish is slow enough for me to follow—or maybe she's slowing down because she can tell I'm struggling. She talks about Havana. About a bakery on Calle Obispo where they made pastelitos "not like these—better, but don't tell the Versailles." About a grandson named Miguel who lives in Union City, New Jersey, and doesn't visit enough.

**Panel 10**
AXEL: (Caption) I don't tell her about the Tape. I don't tell her about Vance. I don't tell her about anything. I stand at the ventanita and eat a pastry that a stranger gave me because I looked hungry, and for twenty minutes the only agenda in the world is guava and small talk.
AXEL: (Caption) The Y-100 signal from the barber shop down the block bleeds into the WQBA from the ventanita radio. Michael Jackson's "Rock with You" fights a losing battle against a Cuban talk-show host shouting about Afghanistan. It sounds like Miami. It sounds like the city I've been living in for twenty-six years without ever stopping to listen.

**Panel 11**
AXEL: (Caption) I walk back to Elio's shop at dusk. The LeMans is on the lift, her belly exposed, transmission pulled and sitting on a concrete floor like a mechanical organ waiting for transplant. Elio is under the car with a trouble light, taking notes on a clipboard.
ELIO: (Dialogue) "She's alive. Barely. But she's alive."
ELIO: (Dialogue) "My cousin runs a fishing boat out of Key West. He might have a donor tranny from a '77 Grand Prix that'll mate to your bell housing."
AXEL: (Dialogue) "How long?"
ELIO: (Dialogue) "Four days. Maybe five. My cousin says there's a lot of boats going back and forth to Cuba right now. More than usual. Something's happening down there, but nobody knows what yet."

**Panel 12**
AXEL: (Caption) I don't ask about the boats. I don't ask about Cuba. I'm standing in a tire shop in Little Havana with guava paste on my shirt and three cafecitos turning my bloodstream into a percussion instrument, and for the first time in fifty-three days, I don't have anywhere to be.
AXEL: (Caption) The candle in the shop window—a Virgin Mary votive, half-burned—flickers in the draft from the open bay door. I watch it for a long time.
AXEL: (Caption) I'm going to need a place to sleep tonight.
