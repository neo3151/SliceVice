# EPISODE 17 SCRIPT: "EARLY BIRD"
**Date:** March 10, 1980
**Historical Anchor:** Hollywood, Florida. Broward County's "Condo Canyon." Between 1970 and 1990, Broward's Jewish population grew from 40,000 to 275,000—primarily retirees from Brooklyn, Queens, and the Bronx fleeing the cold for condominium balconies and the promise of proper rye bread.

---

**Panel 01**
AXEL: (Caption) Day seventy. The LeMans is running clean. Elio's transmission rebuild is holding. Third gear engages like a handshake. Fourth gear hums. The throttle body from the Grand Prix gives her a growl on acceleration that sounds almost intentional—like the car is finally saying something instead of begging.
AXEL: (Caption) I take her north. Shakedown run. Push the new tranny up through the gears on I-95, let the RPMs climb, feel for any slip or shudder. She's not the same car she was on January 1st. That car died somewhere between the Marine Stadium and the Freedom Tower. This is the rebuilt version. The scar tissue version.

**Panel 02**
AXEL: (Caption) I pass the Broward line without meaning to. That's the thing about I-95—the county border is invisible. The road doesn't change. The exits do. HOLLYWOOD BOULEVARD. SHERIDAN STREET. HALLANDALE BEACH BOULEVARD. The names stop sounding Cuban and start sounding like a retirement brochure.
AXEL: (Caption) I take the Hollywood Boulevard exit because the LeMans needs gas and I need a reason to stop running north.

**Panel 03**
AXEL: (Caption) Hollywood Beach. The Condo Canyon. A wall of white and beige high-rise condominiums lining the coast like a concrete fortress built to repel the concept of old weather. Every balcony has a towel draped over the railing and a small American flag. Every parking lot is a sea of Cadillacs and Buicks with New York plates.
AXEL: (Caption) It's 8:45 AM. The beach is empty except for two figures walking at the waterline. The sky is the pale blue of a postcard that nobody bought.

**Panel 04**
AXEL: (Caption) I find a deli on A1A. No name I recognize. Just a storefront with a neon OPEN sign, a counter, and the smell of pastrami and rye bread that hits me in the sinuses like a telegram from a city I've never visited. I sit in a booth—cracked red vinyl, Formica tabletop, laminated menu the size of a newspaper.
AXEL: (Caption) A sit-down meal. My first since... I can't remember the last one. La Carreta on 8th Street, maybe. Before the Tape. Before Vance. Before the math stopped adding up.

**Panel 05**
AXEL: (Dialogue) "Corned beef on rye. Coffee. Eggs, scrambled."
WAITRESS: (Dialogue) "Sour or seedless?"
AXEL: (Dialogue) "I'm sorry?"
WAITRESS: (Dialogue) "The rye, hon. Sour or seedless."
AXEL: (Dialogue) "Sour."
AXEL: (Caption) I don't know why I said that. I've never had a preference about rye bread in my life. But it sounded right, and the waitress nodded like I'd passed a test.

**Panel 06**
SOL: (Dialogue) "The corned beef here is a felony."
AXEL: (Caption) The voice comes from the booth behind me. I turn. He's small—maybe five-four—bald, thick glasses, golf shirt, dress slacks with a crease that could cut paper. He's eating the same thing I ordered, except his plate is half-empty and there's a photograph propped against the sugar dispenser.
SOL: (Dialogue) "I'm Sol. Sol Abramowitz. Formerly of the Bronx—Grand Concourse, if it matters, and it used to. You don't look like you're from around here."
AXEL: (Dialogue) "I'm from Miami."
SOL: (Dialogue) "Nobody's from Miami. Miami is where people end up."

**Panel 07**
AXEL: (Caption) Sol Abramowitz is seventy-four years old. Retired garment-industry foreman. He moved to Hollywood in '77 after his wife Miriam died. He lives in a condo on the eighteenth floor of a building called the Imperial Towers, where the elevator conversations are about the weather in New York and whose grandchild just graduated from something.
SOL: (Dialogue) "My Miriam, she said the corned beef down here was a crime against the Borough of the Bronx. She wasn't wrong. But you eat it anyway, because what else are you going to do—cook?"
AXEL: (Caption) He says this like it's a punchline he's been workshopping for three years. I think it might be.

**Panel 08**
AXEL: (Caption) The food comes. The corned beef is thick, pink, peppery, and stacked too high for the bread to contain it. The rye is warm. The eggs are a little overcooked. It's the best meal I've had in seventy days, and I eat it the way Sol is talking to me—slowly, because there's no reason to rush, and rushing would mean acknowledging that this has to end.
SOL: (Dialogue) "What do you do for a living?"
AXEL: (Dialogue) "Deliveries."
SOL: (Dialogue) "What kind?"
AXEL: (Dialogue) "The kind that keeps the car running."
SOL: (Dialogue) "Ha. You sound like my nephew Lenny. He drove a cab in the city for thirty years. Never owned a thing except the cab. Said the car was the apartment, the office, and the pension. He wasn't wrong either."

**Panel 09**
SOL: (Dialogue) "You want to see something?"
AXEL: (Caption) He reaches into his wallet—carefully, the way a man handles something he's afraid of wearing out—and produces a photograph. Color, slightly faded. A woman in a blue dress standing in front of the Fontainebleau Hotel. 1962, judging by the cars. She's smiling. The wind is in her hair. The light is Miami light—golden, excessive, warm enough to print.
SOL: (Dialogue) "My Miriam. That was '62. She hated Florida. Said the humidity ruined her hair. But she came every winter because I loved it."
SOL: (Dialogue) "She was right about the humidity. She was wrong about coming anyway."
AXEL: (Caption) He puts the photograph back in his wallet with the careful precision of a man who does this gesture fifty times a day. I think about the tape in my own jacket pocket—my mother's voice, on a reel the size of my fist. His proof of another life. My proof of another life. Both of us carrying ghosts in our pockets.

**Panel 10**
AXEL: (Caption) I stay longer than I should. Sol orders pie. He insists I have a slice. The pie is too sweet and the crust is too thick, but I eat it because Sol is watching me eat it with the satisfied intensity of a man who hasn't fed another person in three years.
AXEL: (Caption) The overhead speakers play "Do That to Me One More Time" by Captain & Tennille. Sol hums along absent-mindedly, his fork tracing patterns in the meringue. I catch myself almost smiling. Almost. The muscle memory for it is still there, rusted but functional.
SOL: (Dialogue) "You should come back next week. I'm here every morning. Seven-thirty. Same booth."
**Panel 11**
AXEL: (Caption) I walk out of the deli into the blinding Hollywood morning. The LeMans is parked where I left it. But the passenger door is open.
AXEL: (Caption) Reya is sitting in the passenger seat. She looks exactly the same as she did at the Freedom Tower—leather jacket, grease on her chin, eyes that process the world like a schematic diagram. 
REYA: (Dialogue) "Your transmission sounds better."
AXEL: (Dialogue) "How did you find me?"
REYA: (Dialogue) "I'm a Grid-Engineer. I can track the electromagnetic signature of your alternator from Hialeah. We need to talk."

**Panel 12**
AXEL: (Caption) I get in the driver's seat. The air between us is thick. I haven't seen her since she helped me extract my mother's memory tape.
AXEL: (Dialogue) "Sol was talking about scrap metal disappearing. City buying it up."
REYA: (Dialogue) "It's worse than that. Vance isn't licking his wounds. Vanguard is actively pre-positioning. They're buying up foreclosed property on the coastline in the Drowned District. Mass acquisitions. It doesn't make sense unless he knows an enormous influx of physical bodies is about to hit the shore, and he wants to own the nets they fall into."

**Panel 13**
AXEL: (Caption) I look out the windshield. The ocean is calm. But Reya's right. The math only works if Vance is preparing to harvest thousands of new, desperate souls.
REYA: (Dialogue) "Something big is coming, Axel. The Straits are going to break open. We need to be ready."
AXEL: (Caption) She gets out of the car and walks down A1A, blending into the crowd of retirees like she was never there. 

---

AXEL: (Caption) I drive south on I-95. The LeMans is smooth through the gears. The Miami skyline appears on the horizon—Brickell towers, toothpicks of glass and steel rising out of a haze that could be fog or could be money evaporating.
AXEL: (Caption) WIOD news on the AM dial. Weather. Traffic. Then—

**RADIO (WIOD):** "In local news, Dade County police officers involved in the December 1979 beating death of Arthur McDuffie, a Black insurance salesman and former Marine, have been formally indicted on second-degree murder and manslaughter charges. The trial has been moved to Tampa due to concerns about local jury bias. A date has not yet been set. In other news—"

AXEL: (Caption) I change the station. Power 96 is playing something with a bass line. I turn it up and drive.
AXEL: (Caption) The skyline gets closer. The haze doesn't clear.
